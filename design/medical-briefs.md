## The Situation

The *Meridian* came down hard and the crew came down with it. Three days on, the immediate triage is done — the critical cases are stable, the broken bones are set, the worst of the radiation exposure is logged. You have treated the crash. What you have not yet treated is everything that comes after.

This is not a hospital. You have no resupply, no sterile environment, no backup systems beyond what you salvage. The **Medical department** keeps the crew functional — physically, psychologically, and operationally. Without you, attrition wins. With you, the group has a chance to absorb the losses that are coming and keep moving.

Your base is the **Life Support Section** — a ship fragment that is still circulating air and water, barely, and houses what remains of the medical bay. It is functional if you tend it. It will degrade if you do not.

Medical's disadvantage is that most of what you do only matters when something goes wrong. Your advantage is that something will always go wrong, and when it does, everyone needs you immediately. That access — to every department, in moments of vulnerability — is a form of influence most departments never get.

Every player also carries a **philosophy**: a deeper view of what should happen here. Some of you want to leave. Some want to stay. Some see the crew as a resource to be maintained; others see each person as irreplaceable. You do not know which of the others around you shares your view. That is something you will have to work out.

## Chief Medical Officer
*Head of the Medical Department | Philosophy: Settler*

You are the department head. Four people report to you, and thirty depend on you not making mistakes.

Your public role is triage — not just medical triage, but strategic triage. You decide which injuries are worth the resources to treat fully, which can be managed, which risks are acceptable. You advise Command on crew capacity and tell Exploration when a team is not fit to dive. You are one of the few people whose judgement cuts across every department without requiring permission.

Your private view is that this crew is not going home. Not because you have given up — because you have been paying attention. The reactor situation is worse than Command has told people, the repair window is narrowing, and the planet, as far as you can tell, is survivable if approached carefully. A permanent colony needs healthy people. Your department is not the cleanup crew for a failed evacuation; it is the foundation of something that might actually last.

**Goals**
- Establish a triage protocol within the first turn — clear criteria for which injuries consume resources and which do not.
- Identify the two highest-risk operational conditions on the map and brief Command on them before the midgame.
- Keep crew attrition below the threshold that forces any department to operate below half strength.
- Make the case, with patient data, that the crew's long-term physical condition is better suited to adaptation than to the stress of emergency evacuation.

**Guidance**
- Medical's leverage is information. You know things about crew condition that other departments do not. Decide carefully what you share, with whom, and when.
- The Psych Officer reads the crew's internal state better than anyone. Keep that channel open — ideally before problems become crises.
- Command will want you to clear people for dangerous operations. You can refuse. Do it selectively, but know that the option is always available.
- The Settler philosophy does not mean accepting whatever conditions the planet imposes. It means building something that survives them. Stay focused on what a functioning colony actually requires from its medical infrastructure.

## Med Tech
*Medical Department | Philosophy: Preservationist*


You run the lab. Sample processing, biological assays, pathogen screening, equipment calibration, the Life Support Section's filtration and atmospheric systems — the unglamorous operational core of everything Medical does. The CMO makes the calls; you make the calls possible by giving them accurate data to work from.

The data has been interesting. You are processing biological material from an alien ocean, running it through equipment designed for a different world, and finding things that the manuals have no category for. Some of it is alarming. Some of it is extraordinary. You have started keeping a second log — not for Medical, not for Command — just for yourself, because what this planet is doing to human biology is genuinely remarkable, and you do not want that record to disappear if the group decides to leave.

You want the record to exist either way. You want the planet to be understood before it is stripped or abandoned. You are not sure the others are going to give it that chance.

**Goals**
- Establish a functioning sample analysis pipeline before the midgame — alien biological material in, actionable data out.
- Identify and document at least one novel biological interaction between the planet's ecosystem and human physiology, positive or negative.
- Keep the Life Support Section's atmospheric and filtration systems stable throughout the game; flag any degradation to the CMO before it becomes a crisis.
- Compile a secondary record of ecological and biological findings significant enough to have value independent of the mission's outcome.

**Guidance**
- Ecology is your closest external partner. Their field samples and your lab analysis are more useful combined than separate — build that exchange early and keep it running.
- The Environmental Health Officer is monitoring threats from the outside in; you are monitoring them from the inside out. Compare notes regularly.
- Your secondary log is not secret, but you do not need to advertise it. If someone asks what you are recording, tell them. If nobody asks, keep going.
- Preservationism in Medical is easy to argue: a damaged ecosystem produces fewer of the biological inputs you need to keep people healthy. That is not philosophy — that is your job.
