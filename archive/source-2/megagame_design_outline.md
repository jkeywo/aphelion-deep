# Megagame Design Outline (Working Draft)

## Working Title
Abyssfall

## Core Premise
The players crash-land on an alien ocean world and must survive, explore, and decide whether to repair their ship, build a permanent settlement, or pursue some third path. The main ship’s reactor has sunk deep underwater and will go critical by the end of the game unless it is stabilized, contained, or otherwise resolved.

The game is largely cooperative, but it should create tension between teams through different philosophies and different strategic priorities. The economy is closed. Players must learn to live on the planet by developing sustainable systems inspired by permaculture principles, with a structure loosely influenced by Earthborne Rangers-style interdependence and system building.

---

## Design Goals

- Cooperative survival with meaningful friction
- Exploration driven by risk, discovery, and expansion
- A closed economy with no external resupply
- A visible endgame clock driven by the reactor, with permanent milestone consequences
- Outposts as the main strategic units of play — including as the mechanism for slowing the reactor
- Philosophy as a hidden layer of allegiance and conflict, surfacing through megafauna responses and Drowned tech decisions
- Sustainable systems that become stronger over time
- Deep locations that become stranger, more powerful, and harder to use
- A lost civilisation whose fate mirrors the players' choices
- Megafauna as reactive pressure that creates negotiation, not just jeopardy
- Command authority that is real early and scarce late

---

## Player Experience Arc

The intended game arc is:

1. **Initial confusion and survival**
   - Players identify immediate threats and basic resources
   - Teams begin operating from ship fragments
   - Command distributes early bounties to direct activity

2. **Exploration and expansion**
   - Expeditions discover new locations, resources, and outpost sites
   - Players start building the first sustainable systems
   - First Drowned ruins appear — pure upside, no warnings yet
   - Reactor advances to Phase 2; the Shallows Wanderer activates
   - First philosophical arguments surface around megafauna removal

3. **Strategic divergence**
   - Different philosophies push players toward repair, settlement, exploitation, preservation, or alien contact
   - Outpost choices and resource allocation start to matter strongly
   - Drowned tech becomes available — useful and tempting, with ambiguous warning tokens accumulating
   - The record rooms at Tier 4 begin to reveal what the Drowned discovered and what it cost them
   - Command's bounty hand is running low

4. **Late-game crisis**
   - Reactor advances to Phase 3; the Depth Stalker activates and blocks the reactor site
   - Players face the Drowned tech dilemma: use it to clear the Depth Stalker, or find another way
   - The Drowned revelation completes — players understand what they have activated
   - Philosophy blocs stop being hidden and start negotiating openly
   - The group must commit to a path; the four Drowned response options divide the table

5. **Final resolution**
   - Repair the ship, stabilise the planet-side system, evacuate, contain the Drowned activation, or trigger some alternate ending
   - The shape of the ending reflects what the dominant philosophy bloc built, who they made deals with, and whether the Contained remains caged

## Turn Structure

The game uses a shared mechanical core built around expeditions, outposts, and upkeep.

### 1. Expedition Phase
This is the majority of the turn.

Players arrange themselves into expedition groups and choose where to go. Expeditions may target known locations or attempt to discover new ones.

Expeditions can:
- Explore new terrain
- Find resources
- Reveal outpost locations
- Trigger encounters and hazards
- Discover information, blueprints, or alien systems

Expeditions should have limits:
- Capacity limits
- Equipment limits
- Oxygen or endurance limits
- Risk scaling with depth

This phase is the main driver of discovery and map growth.

### 2. Assignment Phase
Players then select which outpost they will remain at for the turn while upkeep happens.

At this stage:
- Players choose an outpost
- Outposts have limited capacity
- Teams are physically located at specific sites
- Some outposts are better for certain departments or philosophies

### 3. Upkeep and Production Phase
This is resolved simultaneously.

During upkeep:
- Players consume resources at the same time
- Outposts perform exactly one action per turn
- Systems process production, recycling, repair, or special effects
- Reactor pressure advances
- Events may trigger

This creates a strict shared timing structure and forces hard decisions.

---

## Shared Mechanical Core

The core of the game is that:
- Outposts can be built and upgraded once per turn
- Players consume resources simultaneously
- Expeditions occupy the bulk of the turn
- Team time is for assignment, stabilization, and upkeep
- New resources and outpost sites are discovered through exploration
- Deeper outposts become stranger, more powerful, and harder to use

This means the game is not a series of disconnected subgames. Almost every decision should affect outposts, expeditions, or the economy.

## Departments

Departments define what players can do. They are public, operational structures. Philosophy is a separate hidden layer.

Departments vary in size and effectiveness. Each department should have a leader.

### Command (5 players)
**Function:** Coordination, priorities, and global decision-making

**Strengths:**
- Best overall information
- Can allocate shared resources
- Can direct major strategic decisions
- Has influence over the whole board

**Weaknesses:**
- Limited direct production
- Depends on compliance from other departments

**Roles:**
- Mission Director (Leader)
- Operations Officer
- Communications Officer
- Logistics Controller
- Navigator

### Engineering (5 players)
**Function:** Building, repair, infrastructure, reactor interaction

**Strengths:**
- Primary department for repair and construction
- Controls power, fabrication, and structural systems
- Unlocks advanced modules and technical progression

**Weaknesses:**
- Resource intensive
- Dependent on exploration for materials

**Roles:**
- Chief Engineer (Leader)
- Reactor Specialist
- Fabrication Lead
- Power Systems Engineer
- Structural Engineer

### Exploration (5 players)
**Function:** Mapping, discovery, risk-taking, expedition leadership

**Strengths:**
- Reveals the map
- Finds rare resources and new sites
- Has the most freedom of movement

**Weaknesses:**
- High exposure to danger
- Limited long-term infrastructure contribution

**Roles:**
- Expedition Leader (Leader)
- Deep Dive Specialist
- Surveyor
- Xenobiologist
- Salvage Operator

### Ecology / Permaculture (5 players)
**Function:** Sustainability, food, oxygen, closed-loop systems

**Strengths:**
- Enables long-term survival
- Can reduce resource drain
- Stabilizes and improves outpost efficiency
- Shapes sustainable production loops

**Weaknesses:**
- Slow to start
- Needs space, stability, and inputs

**Roles:**
- Lead Ecologist (Leader)
- Systems Ecologist
- Agronomist
- Water Systems Specialist
- Waste/Recycling Engineer

### Medical / Life Support (5 players)
**Function:** Health, resilience, crisis mitigation

**Strengths:**
- Reduces player losses and penalties
- Can stabilize failing systems temporarily
- Supports dangerous expeditions and harsh outposts

**Weaknesses:**
- Reactive rather than proactive
- Dependent on resources and logistics

**Roles:**
- Chief Medical Officer (Leader)
- Trauma Specialist
- Environmental Health Officer
- Psych Officer
- Med Tech

### Corporate / Data (5 players)
**Function:** Information control, data extraction, and off-world interests

**Strengths:**
- Converts discoveries into value
- Gains advantages from scanning and sampling
- Can influence command indirectly

**Weaknesses:**
- Often misaligned with survival priorities
- Limited direct infrastructure capability

**Roles:**
- Corporate Liaison (Leader)
- Data Analyst
- Xenotech Specialist
- Comms Interceptor
- Asset Manager

## Philosophies

Philosophies are hidden. They define what players want to happen, and they are not the same as departments.

Players should start the game not knowing who their allies are. They must discover them through priorities, decisions, and behavior.

### Restorationists
Goal: repair the reactor and escape

Preferred outcomes:
- Reactor progress
- Ship repair
- Recovery of critical systems

### Settlers
Goal: build a permanent, sustainable colony

Preferred outcomes:
- Stable outposts
- Closed-loop systems
- Long-term survival on-planet

### Preservationists
Goal: protect the alien ecosystem

Preferred outcomes:
- Biodiversity
- Minimal exploitation
- Low-impact infrastructure

### Exploiters
Goal: extract maximum value from the planet

Preferred outcomes:
- High throughput
- Rare resources
- Fast growth at ecological cost

### Transcendence
Goal: engage with alien systems and unknown end states

Preferred outcomes:
- Activation of ruins
- Alien interaction
- Experimental or risky breakthroughs

## Role and Philosophy Interactions

The combination of public department and hidden philosophy is the main source of tension.

Examples:
- A Preservationist Engineer may resist reactor repair if it damages the environment
- An Exploiter Ecologist may build sustainable-looking systems only to strip them later
- A Settler in Command may favor stable colonies over direct escape
- A Restorationist in Exploration may prioritize deep reactor access over valuable salvage

This should create discovery, bargaining, and possible deception.

## Outpost System

Outposts are the main strategic structure in the game.

Outposts:
- Can be built and upgraded once per turn
- Have capacity limits
- Have module slots
- Have a base trait tied to their location
- Are the place where long-term progress happens

Players must choose where they are physically stationed during upkeep.

### Starting Outposts
Each department begins with one ship-fragment outpost.

Suggested starting fragments:
- Command: Bridge Fragment
- Engineering: Engine Core Section
- Exploration: Sensor Mast
- Ecology: Hydroponics Bay
- Medical: Life Support Section
- Corporate: Data Core

These should bias the department mechanically, not just narratively.

### Outpost Functions
Outposts can:
- Produce resources
- Process resources
- Host players
- Provide special abilities
- Stabilize systems
- Support expeditions
- Interact with reactor or alien systems

### Outpost Growth
Players can:
- Build new outposts on discovered sites
- Upgrade existing outposts
- Specialize outposts toward a strategic role

## Depth and Outpost Scaling

Deeper outposts should become:
- stranger
- more powerful
- harder to use
- more conditional
- more risky

### Shallow Outposts
- Stable
- Predictable
- Easy to maintain
- Low output
- Good for early permaculture

### Mid-Depth Outposts
- More specialized
- Moderate risk
- Higher output
- Some instability or required inputs

### Deep / Abyssal Outposts
- Very powerful
- Strange and conditional
- Difficult to staff or sustain
- May alter local rules
- May interact with alien systems
- May require continuous attention

Deep outposts should not just produce more. They should introduce unusual rules.

## Permaculture and Closed Economy

The economy should be closed. Players do not receive new resources from outside the system.

### Core Principles
- Resources must be extracted, processed, recycled, or sustained locally
- Overharvesting should reduce future yield
- Good systems should be harder to bootstrap but better in the long run
- Waste should be a major strategic problem

### Suggested Resource Loops
- Food
- Water
- Oxygen
- Biomass
- Soil analogues
- Power
- Materials
- Knowledge

### Suggested System Properties
- Resource conversion chains
- Recycling and waste recovery
- Biodiversity bonuses
- Efficiency gains from integrated systems
- Stress and degradation from overuse

Permaculture should be a mechanically meaningful path, not only a theme.

## Reactor Clock

The reactor is the central endgame pressure.

The reactor has sunk deep underwater and will eventually go critical if not addressed.

### Reactor Phases
1. Dormant leak
2. Instability
3. Critical cascade
4. Meltdown or resolution

### Advancement and Slowdown

The reactor track advances automatically by one step each turn during Upkeep. It does not require anyone to do anything reckless — inaction is sufficient to doom the group. Reckless actions (deep disturbance, Drowned tech use, uncontained outpost construction near the reactor site) may advance it by additional steps.

The track can be slowed — but not reversed — by controlling specific outposts. Generic resource expenditure is not enough. The right infrastructure in the right location is required. This means the question of who controls reactor-relevant outposts is a genuine power question, not just a logistics one.

**Milestone damage is permanent.** Once a phase transition fires, its effect does not go away even if the reactor is later slowed. Players can only prevent the *next* milestone from firing; they cannot undo what has already happened. The only full resolution is a final endgame action.

### Milestone Effects

Each phase transition triggers an immediate, permanent map-wide effect. Different departments feel each milestone at different moments, which staggers the urgency rather than hitting everyone at once.

**Phase 1 → 2 (Dormant to Unstable)**
*The ecosystem layer feels it first.*
- All Tier 1 Mineral cards lose 1 Integrity immediately.
- Radiation tokens are placed on the two deepest currently-explored locations. These tokens remain and accumulate.
- The shallow megafauna stirs: place a Territory token at the Crash Flats.
- Ecology and Medical departments feel this immediately. Others may not notice yet.

**Phase 2 → 3 (Unstable to Critical)**
*The outpost layer feels it.*
- One Tier 2 or Tier 3 location deck gains a Cascade Event card, shuffled in now.
- All Fauna cards at Tier 3 and below lose 1 Population immediately.
- The mid-depth megafauna activates: place it on the map and begin its migration pattern.
- Engineering and Exploration feel this immediately. The reactor access window is visibly closing.

**Phase 3 → 4 (Critical to Cascade)**
*The map layer feels it.*
- The deepest currently-occupied outpost becomes unstable. Players must evacuate it or pay heavy upkeep each turn to hold it.
- The deep megafauna activates and moves to block reactor site access.
- A second Cascade Event card is injected into a Tier 4 location deck.
- The endgame is now mandatory. Everyone feels it.

### Outposts That Can Slow the Reactor

Slowing the reactor requires controlling outposts with specific capabilities. No single department owns all of them — requiring cross-philosophy cooperation to maintain the slowdown.

- **Engineering outpost at the Engine Core Section (upgraded):** Can hold Phase 1→2 for one additional turn per upgrade level.
- **Ecology biological filter outpost (mid-depth):** Reduces radiation token accumulation; can slow Phase 2→3 by one turn if at full capacity.
- **Deep Engineering containment outpost (Tier 3+):** Required to slow Phase 3→4. Cannot be built without Structural Engineer involvement and significant Materials.

Mitigating a late-phase transition will typically require a combination of outpost types controlled by players from opposing philosophy blocs — which is the point.

### Possible Responses
- Repair
- Contain
- Evacuate
- Abandon
- Transform the system through alien means (including Drowned technology — with consequences)

The reactor should not just be a countdown. It should reshape the map and the available options.

Events should:

- Reward resilient systems

## Megafauna

Megafauna are not random encounter tables. They are reactive pressure systems that escalate in response to reactor advancement and ecosystem damage. Control manages them as tools for creating jeopardy and surfacing philosophical conflict — not as punishments.

Three distinct megafauna exist, each tied to a depth tier and triggered by specific conditions. They escalate in danger and complexity. The third may never appear at all.

### The Shallows Wanderer (Tier 1–2)

**Trigger:** Reactor Phase 1→2, or heavy salvage activity at the Crash Flats (Control discretion).

**Behaviour:** Migrating threat. Moves between locations each turn on a predictable path published to players when it activates. Players can see where it is going, which means they can race it, plan around it, or deliberately redirect it toward a rival's expedition route.

**Effect when present:** An expedition entering its current location pays +1 Food. If an outpost is at that location, it cannot perform its upkeep action this turn.

**Removal options — three paths, three philosophical costs:**
- **Redirect** (neutral, free): Spend 1 Biomass to alter its migration path one step. It moves the problem onto someone else. Instant inter-faction conflict. Control notes who redirected it and where.
- **Kill** (fast, costly): Spend 3 Materials and an Engineering action. Permanently removes this megafauna. Also permanently removes one Flora or Fauna card from the current location's ecosystem. Preservationists will object.
- **Appease** (slow, sustainable): Ecology spends 2 Biomass to calm it for one turn. It does not move or block. Repeatable. Leaves the ecosystem intact. Requires Ecology's cooperation — which is not free.

Control notes who argues for which option. This is where hidden philosophy begins to surface.

---

### The Depth Stalker (Tier 3–4)

**Trigger:** Reactor Phase 2→3.

**Behaviour:** Static threat with escalating danger. Does not migrate. Instead, it occupies a deep location and becomes more dangerous each turn it is ignored — gaining a Pressure token each turn, with each token increasing the cost of expeditions to its location.

**Effect when present:** Blocks expedition entry to its location unless the expedition carries sufficient Pressure Rating icons. Each Pressure token on it raises the icon threshold by 1.

**Critical effect:** When the reactor reaches Phase 3→4, the Depth Stalker moves once — directly to the reactor access site. It now blocks reactor repair, containment, or any endgame action at that location.

**The trade-off:** The Depth Stalker can be drawn away from the reactor site by activating Drowned technology. Using Drowned tech creates a signal it responds to, and it will migrate toward the source. This clears the reactor path — but advances the Drowned activation arc. Players must choose between two converging late-game threats.

**Removal options:**
- **Draw away with Drowned tech** (fast, costs Drowned activation progress)
- **Drive off with force** (requires combined Engineering and Exploration expedition, significant resource cost, does not kill it — it returns in two turns)
- **Ecological disruption** (Ecology can collapse the local food web, removing its reason to stay — costly to the ecosystem, one-time permanent removal)

---

### The Contained (Tier 5 — conditional)

**Trigger:** Only activates if Drowned tech activation reaches a critical threshold. May never appear. Its threat value during the game comes entirely from players knowing it could.

**What it is:** Not a natural creature. Something older — possibly engineered, possibly alien — that the Drowned civilisation accidentally awakened and then spent their final years desperately trying to cage. The cage has held for millennia. Drowned tech use is slowly unlocking it.

**Behaviour:** If activated, it does not behave like fauna. It alters the rules of the locations it passes through. Control has a separate brief for managing this scenario.

**Design note:** The Contained is the endgame consequence of ignoring Drowned tech warning signs. It should feel like a category error when it appears — not a bigger version of what came before, but something that operates differently. Its full mechanics are reserved for the Control brief.

### Map Features

### Information Flow
- Exploration controls discovery
- Command controls overall awareness
- Other departments may know only partial information

## Tension Between Cooperation and Conflict

The game should remain cooperative overall, but players should disagree on strategy.

### Strategic Philosophies
- Repair the ship and leave
- Build a sustainable colony
- Protect the ecosystem
- Exploit resources aggressively
- Engage alien systems and unknown outcomes

### Mechanical Sources of Tension
- Limited resources
- Mutually exclusive investments
- Conflicting outpost priorities
- Hidden philosophies
- Shared endgame pressure

This means players can cooperate tactically while opposing one another strategically.

## Exploration Tiers and Biomes

The exploration map is divided into tiers. Each tier has its own biome identity and strategic role.

---

### Tier 1 — Sunlit Shallows
**Role:** Starting zone, safe expansion, basic sustainability  
**Biome identity:** Warm water, high visibility, abundant easy life, fragile ecology

#### 1. Crash Flats
A broad debris field around the wreck.  
**Twist:** High salvage density, but every salvage action increases local hazard and attracts predators.

#### 2. Reef Gardens
Dense coral terraces with slow-growing edible and filtration species.  
**Twist:** The best early permaculture zone, but overharvesting causes long-term yield collapse.

#### 3. Moss Coves
Sheltered coves lined with oxygenating mats and small cave mouths.  
**Twist:** Excellent starting outpost sites, but with limited expansion space.

#### 4. Glass Shoals
A field of transparent silica formations that amplify light and sensor signals.  
**Twist:** Great for scanning and navigation, but reflections can create false readings and ambush risk.

#### 5. Surge Channels
A network of tidal channels cut through the shallow bedrock, fed by forces that shift predictably across the turn cycle.  
**Twist:** Travel is free when the current is running with you, and costly against it. Expeditions that ride the surge arrive fast — but cannot easily return the same turn. Creates real coordination decisions around timing and retrieval.

#### 6. Anemone Shelf
A broad, featureless shelf carpeted in passive filter-feeders that process oxygen and water at scale — the best natural filtration site in the shallows.  
**Twist:** Any outpost construction here disrupts the filtration output. Ecology and Engineering are in direct conflict at this location before they are in conflict anywhere else.

---

### Tier 2 — Kelp and Ruins
**Role:** First expansion tier, moderate danger, early specialization  
**Biome identity:** Vertical forests, moving shadows, wreckage, strong currents

#### 1. Kelp Cathedrals
Towering forests with air pockets and hanging root systems.  
**Twist:** Movement becomes fully three-dimensional, but players can easily get lost and maps become unreliable.

#### 2. Broken Trenchline
A long scar in the seabed filled with half-buried ship sections.  
**Twist:** Wreck fragments function as modular mini-sites, but claiming one may awaken dormant systems or trapped hazards.

#### 3. Current Maze
A network of underwater channels where tides shift travel paths each turn.  
**Twist:** Movement routes change based on current phase or turn order.

#### 4. Blue Ruin Fields
Ancient alien or pre-crash structures, partially grown over by marine life.  
**Twist:** These sites produce knowledge instead of raw materials, but often require a philosophy choice to interpret or activate.

#### 5. Pressure Seam
A long geological boundary where the chemistry, fauna, and pressure of Tier 2 bleed into Tier 3 — the water here is colder, the biology different, the currents unpredictable.  
**Twist:** Acts as an early scout gateway to Tier 3. Expeditions here can gather real data about deeper locations before they are ready to commit to full descent — but information from the Seam is distorted by the transition. Players receive genuine intelligence with uncertain accuracy, and must decide how much they trust what they found.

---

### Tier 3 — Twilight Shelf
**Role:** Midgame transition, where faction differences begin to matter strongly  
**Biome identity:** Dim light, pressure, strange fauna, resource-rich but unstable systems

#### 1. Lantern Drop
A sloping wall filled with bioluminescent organisms and vertical fissures.  
**Twist:** Navigation is visually easy but mechanically tricky; light can reveal hidden hazards or hidden routes.

#### 2. Thermal Vents
Warm vents with fast-growing mineral and microbial ecosystems.  
**Twist:** Strong production site for advanced processing, but every use increases instability or contamination.

#### 3. Echo Basins
Deep basins where sonar and signals bounce unpredictably.  
**Twist:** Communications are distorted here, so Command loses precision and expeditions may return with incomplete reports.

#### 4. Drift Graveyard
A slow-moving field of organic wreckage, dead fauna, and lost equipment.  
**Twist:** High recovery value, but items may carry parasites, corruption, or hidden consequences.

---

### Tier 4 — Blackwater Depths
**Role:** High-risk, high-reward deep exploration  
**Biome identity:** Near-darkness, crushing pressure, rare resources, alien systems

#### 1. Pressure Cathedral
A vast natural chamber with immense pillars and vertical shaft networks — the deepest accessible space before the Abyssal approaches, and the natural territory of the Depth Stalker when active.  
**Twist:** Supports powerful outposts, but only if staffed and maintained every turn or it destabilizes. When the Depth Stalker is present, every expedition here risks a severe escalation event.

#### 2. Bone Fields
A plain of enormous skeletal remains from extinct or ancient creatures, with the highest density of recoverable deep-site materials in the accessible ocean — ancient remains, drifted wreck fragments, and mineral deposits accumulated over centuries.  
**Twist:** Great source of rare materials and biochemical knowledge, but harvesting may anger apex fauna or violate preservation rules.

#### 3. Black Coral Maze
A maze of slow-growing black coral towers that block line of sight and sensor range.  
**Twist:** Hidden routes and hidden nodes are common, but teams can become separated and stranded.

---

### Tier 5 — Abyssal / Alien Core
**Role:** Endgame tier, where the reactor problem and the planet’s own mystery converge  
**Biome identity:** Almost no natural light, alien geometry, impossible ecology, reality-bending effects

#### 1. The Living Vent
A geothermal and biological hybrid site that seems partly organic and partly engineered, descending through vertical shaft networks toward the reactor’s resting place. The most direct route to the endgame reactor site passes through here.  
**Twist:** It can produce nearly any advanced resource, but only if players maintain a delicate ecological balance. Sustained deep operations within the Vent advance the endgame clock — every expedition toward the reactor requires passing through what is also the world’s most fragile deep ecosystem.

#### 2. The Cold Eye
A still, silent basin at the deepest point, with no obvious life and impossible readings. Alien structures — not the Drowned’s work — are present here, growing slowly through the rock in patterns no survey fully maps.  
**Twist:** This is a revelation site. It does not give many materials, but it reveals the final truth needed to resolve the reactor, the planet, or both. Engaging deeply with the alien structures here grants significant capability upgrades — but each engagement may alter the rules of outposts in surrounding locations, in ways that cannot be predicted in advance.

---

## Suggested Dominant Question by Tier

- **Shallows:** Can we survive?
- **Kelp / Ruins:** Can we map and adapt?
- **Twilight:** Can we specialize without collapsing?
- **Blackwater:** Can we exploit without losing control?
- **Abyssal:** Can we finish the mission without breaking the world?

## The Drowned

Somewhere in the deep, below the reactor's resting place, there is evidence of a prior civilisation. They built here. They thrived here. They are gone.

The Drowned are not background lore. They are a mechanical arc with a dramatic payoff. Their ruins contain genuine technology — useful, powerful, tempting. Their technology also contains the reason they died. Players will discover one before they discover the other.

### What the Drowned Were

An advanced civilisation that inhabited this ocean world long before the Meridian's arrival. They mastered the same depth tiers players are now exploring. They built outposts at Tier 4 and deeper. They discovered something in the abyss — an entity, a system, a signal — and they activated it. The activation gave them extraordinary capability. It also, eventually, destroyed them.

The Drowned did not fail through ignorance. They failed because they could not agree on what to do once they understood what was happening. The record rooms in Tier 4 make this clear to anyone who reads them carefully.

### Discovery Arc

The Drowned reveal unfolds in three stages across the depth tiers.

**Stage 1 — Ruins and artefacts (Tier 2–3)**
Players find structures. Knowledge cards from these locations carry fragments of Drowned understanding: architectural techniques, biological insights, power systems unlike anything from the Meridian. This is pure upside. Players feel clever and rewarded for going deep. No warnings yet.

**Stage 2 — The record rooms (Tier 4)**
Specific Knowledge cards, when drawn, reveal what happened. The revelations should be fragmented — no single card tells the whole story. Players must compare notes, share cards, and piece together the picture. This creates information asymmetry: some players understand what they are looking at before others do. Knowledge becomes a trading commodity.

The record rooms also contain the first clear warnings about what Drowned tech does to the ecosystem and what it is slowly unlocking. At the moment these cards are drawn, Control should already be watching — whatever the players are currently doing wrong, escalate it slightly.

**Stage 3 — The cage (Tier 5)**
The deepest Drowned structures are not ruins. They are a containment system, still active, for something the Drowned spent their final years trying to imprison. The entity they activated — the Contained — is still in there. Drowned tech use has been slowly weakening the cage. Players arriving at Tier 5 will find out how much damage they have already done.

### Drowned Technology

Drowned tech cards are found as Knowledge cards in Tier 3–5 decks. They function as powerful hand cards — more capable than anything the players brought with them.

**Using Drowned tech produces two simultaneous effects:**
1. An immediate small ecosystem damage effect — a specific Flora or Mineral card at the current location loses 1 token.
2. A visible but ambiguous warning token placed on the map by Control. These tokens accumulate. Ecology can read them; others may dismiss them.

Early in the game, the damage is easy to rationalise. The token is easy to ignore. This is intentional.

As Drowned tech use accumulates, the warning tokens cluster. The ecosystem damage compounds. At a defined threshold — tracked privately by Control — the Contained begins to stir. Control introduces escalating Signal Events drawn from the Drowned brief.

### The Final Decision

Once players understand what they have activated — through the fragmented Tier 4 revelations and Control's Signal Events — they face a genuine choice with no obviously correct answer. Each path is viable. Each maps to a different philosophy bloc.

- **Stop using it entirely** — lose the benefits, slow the activation, accept the cost. Preservationists push for this.
- **Contain and stabilise** — spend significant resources to manage the damage without abandoning the tech. Settlers push for this.
- **Use more to control it** — lean into Drowned systems to master what was activated. Exploiters and some Transcendence players push for this.
- **Race the reactor** — accept the Drowned damage as a known cost and resolve the reactor before the Contained fully activates. Restorationists push for this.

This decision should be the most contested negotiation in the game. Control should time the forced choice for maximum philosophical conflict — ideally when the reactor is already at Phase 3 and the Depth Stalker is blocking the reactor site.

### Drowned Tech and the Depth Stalker

The Depth Stalker responds to Drowned tech signals. Activating Drowned technology will draw it away from whatever location it is currently occupying — including the reactor site. This creates a direct mechanical incentive to use Drowned tech even for players who understand the risk. The short-term benefit (clearing the reactor path) directly accelerates the long-term threat (the Contained). Control should never need to force this dilemma — the game structure creates it automatically.

## Negotiation and Command Authority

### Deals

All deals in Abyssfall are verbal and unenforceable. There are no formal trade cards, no mechanical enforcement, no penalty printed on a card for betrayal. Reputation is the only currency of trust, and it accumulates — or depletes — through play.

This means:
- Resource trades are fast and informal. Betrayal is possible and interesting.
- Outpost access promises, philosophy commitments, and expedition arrangements are also verbal. Breaking them has social consequences, not mechanical ones.
- Players who build reputations for reliability accumulate real influence. Players who betray deals become known for it.

Control does not arbitrate deals. They observe them.

### Command's Role

Command begins the game with the best overview of what every department can do and what the overall situation requires. They are the natural brokers — not because they have authority to compel, but because they have information everyone else needs and a structural incentive to coordinate the group.

**Command's primary leverage tool is the Bounty Hand.**

At game start, each Command player receives a hand of Bounty cards. These are equipment upgrades, outpost modules, and capability cards — items already in the card economy, representing real mechanical value. Command can offer them as prizes for achievements they want to incentivise: reaching a specific location, completing a resource chain, delivering a report, staffing a critical outpost.

**Bounty card rules:**
- Command receives their full hand at game start. There is no replenishment.
- Bounties are offered publicly or privately — Command's choice.
- The first player or team to complete the stated achievement claims the card.
- Command must spend their bounties wisely. They will run out before the game ends, and the late game is when their leverage is most needed.

This creates a natural power arc: early Command is flush and influential, directing activity across the board. Late Command is scraping for leverage just as the reactor and Drowned crises peak. The group's ability to coordinate in the endgame depends partly on whether Command spent their bounties well.

### Philosophy Blocs as Negotiating Parties

As the game develops, hidden philosophies become open secrets. Players stop pretending and start negotiating as blocs. The late game is not primarily a logistics problem — it is a political one.

The reactor milestone responses require specific outpost types controlled by specific players. Whoever controls those outposts has leverage over the whole group's survival options. Philosophy blocs that have been building quietly since turn one now reveal what they have constructed — and what they want in exchange for using it.

The most interesting late-game deals are not about resources. They are about the shape of the ending.

## Important Design Risks

### Outpost Spam
Players may try to maximize outpost count.

Possible controls:
- Upkeep increases with number of outposts
- Staffing requirements
- Diminishing returns on shallow sites
- Resource pressure from expansion

### Idle Time
Expedition-heavy play can leave players waiting.

Possible controls:
- Parallel expedition resolution
- Micro-roles within expeditions
- Department-level upkeep tasks

### Dominant Strategy: Go Deep Immediately
Deep locations may become obviously best if not constrained.

Possible controls:
- Prerequisites for access
- Instability costs
- Staffing demands
- Ecological or technical gating

### Weak Philosophy Impact
If philosophy does not change actual decisions, it becomes cosmetic.

Possible controls:
- Distinct scoring tied to system outcomes
- Philosophy-linked incentives
- Endgame reveal and scoring
- Soft win conditions for more than one philosophy

### Drowned Tech as Obvious Best Option
If Drowned tech is powerful enough, players may use it regardless of warning signs — treating the warning tokens as an acceptable tax.

Possible controls:
- Warning token effects must compound visibly and painfully (not just minor ecosystem damage)
- The Depth Stalker's attraction to Drowned tech signals must feel like an immediate, meaningful consequence — not a future abstraction
- The Contained's activation threshold should not be publicly known; uncertainty is the pressure

### Reactor Slowdown Captured by One Bloc
If a single philosophy bloc controls all reactor-relevant outposts early, they can either hold the reactor hostage or solve it unilaterally, collapsing the negotiation dynamic.

Possible controls:
- Reactor slowdown outposts should require inputs from at least two departments to build and maintain
- No single outpost should be sufficient to slow the reactor alone — combination is always required
- Control should monitor outpost concentration and inject events that challenge monopoly positions

### Megafauna Becoming Irrelevant
If players quickly develop the resources to kill every megafauna, the reactive pressure system loses its teeth.

Possible controls:
- The Depth Stalker cannot be permanently killed — only driven off or redirected
- Killing the Shallows Wanderer should have an ecosystem cost significant enough that Ecology actively resists it
- The Contained is explicitly not removable through conventional means

## Summary
The game is a cooperative-but-divided megagame about surviving a crash on an alien ocean world. Players operate through departments, but their secret philosophies create hidden alliances and conflicts. The main gameplay loop is expedition, discovery, outpost development, and upkeep under a tightening reactor clock. The economy is closed, sustainability is central, and the deepest parts of the world offer power at a cost.

The game has three converging pressure systems: the reactor clock (automatic, permanent, outpost-controlled), the Drowned activation arc (player-driven, tempting, dangerous), and the megafauna (reactive, philosophically charged, escalating). These systems are designed to interact — using Drowned tech draws the Depth Stalker away from the reactor but advances the Contained's awakening. Slowing the reactor requires cross-philosophy cooperation. Killing megafauna damages the ecosystems that Ecology depends on. Every decision ramifies.

Command holds the group together through bounties and information — but their leverage runs out before the endgame arrives. The final turns should feel like barely-controlled chaos in which previously hidden alliances become open, and the shape of the ending is decided by who built what, where, and who they owe a favour to.
